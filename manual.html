How to use this manual

You do not need it. The game teaches itself, eventually; one of our playtesters can confirm it takes about thirty runs. This manual is for the other kind of engineer: the kind who reads the runbook before the incident. Chapters 1–3 get you playing. Chapters 4–9 explain why you lost. The appendices are reference, generated from the game itself, so they are true even when this prose is out of date.

Look for Field Notes in the margins: tips from the engineers who came before you. They are honest, if not always specific. For a complete walkthrough, see the Official Strategy Guide (sold separately; see the back cover).

Chapter 1Welcome aboard

You are the last engineer awake. Version 1.0 ships tonight.

Dependency Hell is an action game about the software you install. You fight your way through rooms full of bugs, and every room is a piece of software your application depends on. You will need help. Help comes from the package registry.

Every package you install gives you a power the moment you install it: rapid fire, bouncing shots, a drone, a shield. Every package is also a dungeon: it brings its own dependencies, and they bring theirs, and every one of them becomes a room on your map. The more you install, the stronger you get, and the bigger the thing you are responsible for.

Rooms you have cleared are vetted: you have read the code, as it were, and it holds. Rooms you have not are liabilities. Every few rooms the pager goes off, and something you never looked at goes down, taking every package that depends on it with it. Your powers go dark mid-fight. That is the game.

Ship three features, then walk into the deploy door and face production: a boss built from your own architecture, which strikes every corner you cut.

Field Note The central joke is that you are building the maze while trying to escape it. It stops being a joke around room nine.

Chapter 3Your first sprint

  1. Install. In app, your home room, walk to the REGISTRY terminal and press E. It offers three packages. Each card shows the power, the stronger hardened version of it, the dependency tree it will drag in, and what it costs. Take one. Its power is yours immediately.
  2. The graph grows. The package and its whole tree appear on the map as rooms. Doors on the east wall lead deeper into the tree; doors on the west wall lead back toward app.
  3. Integrate. Walk into the package's own room and clear it. That ships a feature.
  4. Vet, or don't. Every dependency room you clear is vetted. When a package's entire tree is vetted, the package is hardened: a stronger version of its power.
  5. Keep going. The registry restocks every few rooms; you may take one package per restock. Every room you clear moves the release train one step.
  6. Deploy. With three features shipped you may walk into the deploy door, on the west wall of app. When the clock runs out you must.
Field Note Server racks stop bullets. You will learn this eventually. You could learn it now.
Field Note Some packages are better together. A shot that bounces is good. Three shots that bounce are a different game.

Chapter 4The graph is the dungeon

Every node in your dependency graph is a room, and every edge is a door. Press Tab to see it all: you can select any node and fast-travel to it.

Node states

□ Unvetted
Never cleared. A liability: the pager can take it down, and production will strike it.
■ Vetted
Cleared. It holds: the pager passes it by and production's strikes bounce off it.
■ Down
Taken down by an incident. Every package that depends on it, directly or through other nodes, goes offline: its power switches off until the node is back up.
▢ Hardened
A package whose whole tree is vetted. Its power is stronger.

Getting in

You can enter a room if any node that requires it has been vetted. A door marked LOCKED means nothing upstream of that room has been cleared yet: work your way down from the top.

Shared nodes

Packages share dependencies. That is efficient (a shared node is one room, not two) and dangerous: one outage there takes down every package that leans on it. The registry warns you when a new package would share a node with what you have.

Version conflicts

Two packages that need different major versions of one library conflict. In every fight, one side of the conflict fails to load, at random. The library's room also gets a Version Conflict to fight (chapter 11). Vet both versions and the conflict resolves itself; or buy a Compat Shim to paper over it, for debt.

Risk and flags

Some nodes are riskier than others (▲ risky, ☠ very). The pager prefers them. Some carry a flag: a known CVE (ship it unvetted and production takes it personally), abandoned, license trouble, or unpublishable. The last is special: an unpublishable package can be pulled by its author no matter how well you vetted it. Only a pin protects it.

One package is special in another way. It grows.

From the map

Select a node, then: Enter travel there; H hotfix it if it is down; P pin it; C containerize it; X uninstall a package (from app only).

Field Note The registry shows you the tree before you install it. Read it. Count the rooms. Count your rooms.

Chapter 5Pressure: the train, the pager and debt

The release train

Every room you clear is one step on the clock. Greenfield gives you fourteen. The registry restocks every three rooms. You will rarely have time to install much and vet all of it, which is the point. The sidebar shows rooms left beside rooms still unvetted; when the second number is bigger, you cannot vet everything any more, and should choose.

The pager

Every three rooms (every two once your debt reaches 50), the pager takes down one unvetted node, chosen at random but preferring risky and flagged ones. It picks its target one room ahead. With observability 2 or better you are told which node it is: vet it in time and the incident is averted, worth three commits. Pinned nodes are skipped.

Outages

A down node stays down until you clear its room, now an incident room with a tougher fight, or hotfix it from the map: two commits and ten debt, and the node comes back up still unvetted. A Container on a node stops its outages from spreading to the packages that depend on it.

Technical debt

Debt comes from shortcuts: shims, hotfixes, pins, feature flags, energy drinks, forced installs, some packages, and features you deploy without. It does three things. Every 25 points makes rooms bigger and their bugs more often elite. At 50 the pager speeds up. All of it makes production tougher. A Refactor pays down 20. Nobody will thank you.

Observability

1Where you start. Enemy health bars, risk markers on the map, the first level of a tree in the registry.
2Pager forecasts: which node goes down next. Whole trees and exact version conflicts in the registry.
3Warning rings on bugs about to fire, and a tell on things that are not what they seem.

Raise it with a Test Suite, or with packages that add it. Deploy at 1 and you may meet the boss whose health you cannot see.

Field Note The pager is not random about what it hits. It is random about when you notice.

Chapter 6Change freeze

When the last room on the clock is cleared, the release train leaves and a change freeze begins.

Frozen
Installing packages and refreshing the registry. Entering any unvetted room, which means no more vetting, and no integrating a package you installed but never shipped.
Still allowed
Incident response. A node that is down can be traveled to and its room fought; clearing it brings the node back. Hotfixes and the Rollback Plan work as usual, and tools can still be bought.
Silent
The pager. Nothing new goes down once the train has left, including on the room that started the freeze.
Required
Deploying. It is the only way forward. Every feature short of three adds ten debt and makes production a quarter angrier.

A down node behind rooms you never vetted cannot be reached during a freeze. Hotfix it from the map, roll back, or ship without it.

Field Note A freeze stops changes. It has never once stopped an incident.

Chapter 7Production

The deploy door asks for confirmation and shows you what production will find: your features, your weak points, and who is waiting. Then you fight it.

Production is built from your run

Its identity comes from whatever dominates your architecture. Its health scales to your own gun (damage, shots, critical hits, fire rate) at the moment you deploy, and to your debt. Separate shooters and on-hit effects (drones, orbitals, bounces, blasts) are not counted against you.

Strikes

As its health drops, production strikes. Each strike hits one of your weak points: an unvetted node goes down (and every power that depends on it goes dark, mid-fight), a shim breaks, a pinned version is forced to upgrade. Strikes on vetted nodes visibly bounce off. This is where every shortcut you took comes due.

Field Note A Rollback Plan works in production too. Nobody has ever regretted bringing one.

Chapter 8Tools of the trade

Commits buy tools at the registry. Press a tool's key, or click it; after buying, its instructions appear under the list. Tools that are charges (Pin, Container, Rollback Plan) are bought now and used later: pins and containers from the map, rollbacks with R in a fight.

Field Note Saving commits for the end is a strategy. So is spending them all on Refresh Registry until the right package shows up. Both have won.

Chapter 9Postmortems and grades

Every run ends in a report, built only from what actually happened: every line in it can be traced to an event in your run.

A run that fails ends in a postmortem: the root cause, the contributing factors, what went well, action items, and, first, Next sprint, try: one or two things that would most likely have saved this particular run.

A release that ships gets a release report and a grade, scored out of seven:

Measured at release2 points1 point0
Stability left60+30–59under 30
Unvetted nodes01–34+
Technical debtunder 2525–4950+
Production strikes that landed—noneany

S 7 · A 6 · B 4–5 · C 2–3 · D 0–1 · F did not ship

The report shows the breakdown, and what the next point needed.

Field Note An S is a flawless release: nothing unvetted, nothing struck, nothing owed. It has been done. Legal was suspicious.

Chapter 11A field guide to bugs

Which bugs a room spawns depends on what kind of software it is: crypto libraries breed race conditions, legacy code breeds deprecated APIs, and so on. Elites are tougher versions of the same bug; debt and incidents make them more common.

Appendix CTroubleshooting

I press Q and nothing happens.
The feature flag comes with the launch-dorkly package. Without it there is no flag to flip, and the game will say so.
A door says LOCKED.
Nothing that requires that room has been vetted yet. Clear the room above it first.
A door says CHANGE FREEZE.
The release train has left. Only down nodes can be entered now. Chapter 6.
My power went dark in the middle of a fight.
Something it depends on is down, or it lost a version conflict this fight. The packages panel says which.
I vetted left-pad and it still went down.
It was unpublished. Vetting cannot stop that; a pin can.
A package I hardened is not hardened any more.
It grew. Go and read what it calls now.
The boss was much tougher than last time.
It scales to your gun and your debt, and every feature you deployed without makes it angrier.
A commit bit me.
It was not a commit.
I keep losing.
Read the first section of your postmortem. Then read it again. Then remember that one of our playtesters lost thirty-two runs in a row and then shipped an S.
